Dear Editors,

Please consider the enclosed manuscript, "An information-theoretic exposure criterion for AI-denoised low-dose CT: from required detectability to delivered dose, and to an AI denoiser's dose claim", as a Research Paper in AI Horizons.

AI denoising is widely held to improve image quality, evidenced by reference-based fidelity metrics, and thereby to permit dose reduction. The second claim does not follow from the first. A denoiser sees only the image it is given, so the hypothesis, the input and the processed image form a Markov chain, and no test on the processed image dominates the likelihood-ratio test on the input. That bound says nothing about whether a given observer recovers what the input holds, and nothing about exposure. The manuscript closes that gap: it separates the detectability an input makes available to an ideal observer from the detectability a specified observer actually achieves, and converts a stated detection requirement into the exposure a protocol needs. The result is a criterion a reader can apply to an AI denoiser's dose claim rather than a figure of merit to compare against another figure of merit. We submit it to AI Horizons because its subject is the reliability and the limits of a learned method, tested against a bound rather than against a benchmark, which is what the journal asks for of AI in science and engineering.

Two arms support it. A controlled synthetic matrix isolates the mechanism across dose, noise correlation and lesion configuration, with three classical denoisers and three observers, each referenced to its own unprocessed arm and to that input's analytic ideal-observer ceiling. A real arm repeats the measurement on twelve Siemens liver cases from LDCT-and-Projection-data, with a synthetic lesion of known size and contrast inserted into real abdominal soft tissue and detection scored by a cross-fitted prewhitening observer on paired trials, so that the anatomy cancels and the noise does not.

Three results seem to us worth a reader's time. Converted into exposure, the unprocessed input met the prespecified criterion down to 0.187 of the routine protocol, and seven of the eight processed arms required more exposure than that to meet the same criterion; the exposure implied by a PSNR gain reached 2.04 times the exposure actually delivered, and 4.8 times at a dose where the task had already failed. Across nine methods on a thousand held-out pairs from four cases no network ever saw, the method with the best PSNR ranked seventh of nine on the detection task, and no arm exceeded the closed-form ceiling of its own input. At each of two capacities a second network was trained that differs from the first in the objective and in nothing else, by a least-squares adversarial term, so that an arm capable of fabricating structure exists rather than being assumed away; none of the processed arms produced more lesion-shaped response in a lesion-free image than the noise itself carries, and the adversarial arm at 1.85 M parameters instead erased more of a real lesion than any other method.

We have tried to be explicit about what the study does not establish, and the manuscript carries that paragraph rather than leaving it to this letter. The lesion is inserted rather than native, so what is measured is detection of a specified low-contrast signal and not of hepatocellular carcinoma, hemangioma or any native lesion. No human reader was involved. The dose axis is a simulation anchored on a measured noise realization, and the manuscript says which part of it is measured and which modeled. The adversarial result is a null from a measure that cancels the anatomy and therefore cannot see structure invented from the anatomy alone, and it is reported as that rather than as an absence of fabrication. The adversarial arms and the exposure conversion were added after the first results and are labeled as analyses rather than as tests of a prespecified hypothesis.

Every number in the manuscript is emitted by executed code into machine-readable files and resolved into the text at build time; the test suite fails if the two disagree, so no value can be typed into the prose. The software is public now, not upon acceptance: denoiq-core at github.com/Institute-of-One/denoiq-core, archived as version 0.2.0 (doi:10.5281/zenodo.23095236), and ldct-io at github.com/Institute-of-One/ldct-io, archived as version 0.2.0 (doi:10.5281/zenodo.23095235).
